What this means

The accessibility score is not a percentage of elements that pass. It is a pass rate across WCAG 2.2 success criteria: each criterion — such as “images have text alternatives” or “text has sufficient colour contrast” — is either met or not met on the pages scanned. A criterion failed by one element counts the same as a criterion failed by a hundred, because that is how conformance actually works: no amount of unrelated passing markup earns back a failed criterion.

Criteria are weighted by how strictly they matter: Level A criteria (the baseline most legal claims cite) carry a weight of 3, Level AA criteria (the common ADA/EAA conformance target) carry a weight of 2, and best-practice checks that sit outside formal WCAG criteria carry a weight of 1. Your overall score then blends accessibility (60%) with SEO (40%), reflecting how much each tends to affect legal exposure and organic visibility.

The element counts in the severity breakdown above are a different measurement: they indicate the size of the remediation job, not the score itself. A criterion can be failed by five elements or five hundred — either way it is one failed criterion, but five hundred is a bigger fix.

Needs manual review

On its own, automated testing is a floor, not a ceiling. Tools like this one — including the axe-core engine behind it — reliably catch roughly a third to a half of real-world accessibility barriers: broken alt text, missing labels, insufficient contrast, and structural markup problems. They cannot judge whether a screen-reader user can actually complete checkout, whether focus order makes sense, or whether a video's captions are accurate. A conformance claim requires manual testing and assistive-technology testing on top of what is in this report, not instead of it.
